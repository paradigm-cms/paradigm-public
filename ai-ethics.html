00 · The premise

Why This Matters

AI that helps us decide, not AI that decides for us. Each office can set where that line falls for its own work.

01 · The work in front of us

Each Office Should Define Ethical AI for Itself.

What do we mean when we say:

“Prosecution cannot be automated.”

AI has been added to more and more of the digital tools in prosecutors’ offices. If each tool automates its own part of the work, the office may reach a point where prosecution is no longer being done by the office.

Budget authorities (local, state, federal) may also reduce staffing on the expectation that AI makes the work faster. That expectation should be tested against what the tools do.?Open questionAt what point does automating parts of the work change who is doing prosecution, and who decides?

Which parts of the work must stay with people?

02 · The challenge of judgment

The Ship of Theseus.

The line is between AI that helps us decide and AI that decides for us.

One Plank at a Time

Plutarch reports that the Athenians preserved the ship Theseus sailed home and “took away the old timbers from time to time, and put new and sound ones in their places,” until philosophers disputed whether it was still the same vessel (Life of Theseus 23). Our office is a Ship of Theseus: its planks are the tools it uses, and each can be replaced by a different AI tool, one at a time.

This is a sorites-style puzzle: no one plank stops it being a prosecutor's office. The worry assumes every step is alike. We argue the step that crosses the line hands a constitutive judgment to the tool. An office could keep its title and staff and no longer be doing prosecution.

Constitutive, Not Merely Characteristic

The prosecutor’s role is set out in Berger v. United States: the prosecutor represents “a sovereignty” whose interest “is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done.” ABA Model Rule 3.8, comment [1], calls this the responsibility of “a minister of justice and not simply that of an advocate.”

Constitutive
Judgments That Make It Prosecution
  • Whether to bring, proceed on, amend, or dismiss a charge, including grand jury presentment
  • Declining or diverting
  • Plea offers
  • Disclosure determinations
  • Pretrial-detention and bail positions
  • Sentencing recommendations

Test: the act counts only when an officeholder who can give its reasons makes it, and its point is justice in the case.

Characteristic
Tasks Typical of the Work
  • Status messages and scheduling
  • Boilerplate drafting
  • Extraction with page citations
  • Retrieval for a person to read
  • Summaries that stay traceable to the source

Test: anyone or anything can do it, provided its output, including what it left out, can be checked against the record.

In some jurisdictions police or a magistrate starts a charge, and the office decides whether it goes forward. The test adapts Searle’s constitutive rules, which create an act, and MacIntyre’s internal goods, which only the practice provides.

Human at the Helm

Alex Pearson contrasts the lawyer “in-the-loop,” who reviews output after the work is done, with the lawyer “at-the-helm”, who sets direction before the agent starts (National Law Review, May 8, 2026). His no-go zones are set by stakes. We go further: for constitutive judgments the prosecutor is the source of the judgment. Capability cannot change that; only the law that defines the office could, and then the office would be prosecution in name only. The office acts through named people who can give the decision’s reasons and answer for it: the chief prosecutor (elected or appointed), who sets written policy; supervisors, who review; and the prosecutor who decides the case.

ABA guidance agrees: “lawyers may not abdicate their responsibilities by relying solely on a GAI tool to perform tasks that call for the exercise of professional judgment” (ABA Formal Op. 512, 2024).

Phronesis, Aristotle’s practical wisdom, is exercised through choice, and he places the origin of choice in the person who acts (Nicomachean Ethics VI.2). Advisers help, but the person chooses (III.3). A person is answerable for a judgment when it is open to a demand for their reasons (Shoemaker, Ethics 121(3), 2011). Being held to account means being liable to blame or sanction for what was done (Watson, “Two Faces of Responsibility,” Philosophical Topics 24(2), 1996). A sign-off can leave a person held to account for a judgment whose reasons were never theirs. Adopting a tool’s framing makes it the prosecutor’s reason only if the prosecutor can state and defend it from the record.

Human-in-the-loop remains the name of a review step. It is necessary, not sufficient.

The Frame
Human at the Helm

The prosecutor originates constitutive judgments; tools inform them.

The Standard
Meaningful Human Control

Tracking: the system responds to the relevant people’s reasons. Tracing: outcomes lead back to a person who understands the system and their role in it.

The Failure
Moral Crumple Zone

A sign-off carries responsibility for a decision the person had little control over.

Where the Removed Planks Go

Hobbes added a variant: a second ship, rebuilt from the removed planks, with an equal claim to be the original. Hobbes held that, in questions of identity, “we must consider by what name anything is called” (De Corpore II.11.7). By that test, a thing identified by its material becomes another thing when the material is replaced, and a thing identified by its activity persists while the activity continues. Applied here: an office is named for its work, prosecution, so it stays one only while people in it exercise the constitutive judgments. The question is who holds a removed judgment now, and who answers for it.

04 · The consequences of unchecked output

The Consequences of Unchecked Output.

Since 2023, courts have sanctioned or rebuked lawyers, including government lawyers, over filings with citations an AI tool had invented. Three recent examples follow.

Oct 2025 · Nevada County, CA
DA office faces a Supreme Court–ordered sanctions inquiry over AI errors

In the criminal case People v. Turner, the DA's response to a habeas petition challenging a defendant's pretrial detention cited three nonexistent cases and three that did not support the points made. In January 2026 the CA Supreme Court unanimously ordered the Court of Appeal to consider sanctions; as of September 2026 a court-appointed referee was investigating.

Kjoller v. Superior Court · People v. Turner · ABA Journal, Nov 2025 · Hoodline, Sep 2026
Mar 2026 · E.D.N.C.
Assistant U.S. Attorney fired over AI fabrications

An AUSA defending the Department of Defense in a civil case was fired after filing a brief with AI-fabricated citations and quotes. The Magistrate Judge called the filings "particularly odious" and limited the sanction to a public reprimand.

Fivehouse v. DoD · ABA Journal, Apr 2026 · Bloomberg Law
Jan 2025 · Minnesota AG
Attorney General's expert declaration excluded

In a case about deepfakes, the Minnesota Attorney General's office filed an expert's declaration containing AI-hallucinated citations: two nonexistent articles and a third misattributed. The court excluded the declaration and reminded counsel of their Rule 11 duties.

Kohls v. Ellison, D. Minn. · Greenberg Traurig analysis

In each case, the filing went out without a check against the sources.

05 · Our strategy

Our Strategy

Prosecutors see how pressure affects judgment and how careful people can drift one small choice at a time. A strategy for AI has to account for that.

01 · Work together
Collaborate.
Early. Concretely. Openly.

The answer is not settled. The goal is sound practice for our communities and the offices we serve, so we collaborate early, concretely, and in the open.

02 · The oath
The duty comes first.
We build with the prosecutor's duty in view.

We build toward tools that respect the prosecutor's oath and keep human decisions with people.

03 · Our policy
Communicate.
In plain terms.

State the policy in plain terms. Move deliberately: consider how a feature can fail, and how an error in our technology could harm a case or a person.

04 · Our line
Keep the decision with people.
Human at the helm. No chatbot decisions.

Human at the helm: the office decides, through named people who can give the decision’s reasons and answer for it. No chatbot answers a question only a prosecutor should decide. Chat is acceptable for configuration help and for drafting walled off from case data. This can make some work slower.

11 · What this unlocks

Useful Tools. Less Risk.

These are the gains the design standard is built to deliver.

Payoff 01
More data, less burden.

Richer, more accurate data extracted from the documents you already collect, without adding hours to your paralegals' day. The aim is that capacity spent on data entry can go back to the work that needs a human.

Payoff 02
Configuration without change orders.

Tags, metrics, and workflows are configured by your own team instead of requested from the vendor as change orders. The aim is that an improvement does not need a budget line.

Payoff 03
Templates over drafts. Agency intact.

Office-approved templates carry the boilerplate. Prosecutors write the human-necessary parts. AI flags places to double-check, without writing in your voice. The aim is fewer errors with the prosecutor's agency intact.

Payoff 04
Protection: office, staff, and field.

Under the design standard, your office is less likely to make a public error from unchecked AI output. Staffing decisions can draw on the Value ratings in How to Rate an AI Feature and the deskilling cost in Our Conversation.

Where the line falls between AI that serves justice and AI that replaces it will not be settled by any one office. The decision boundary

Which decisions are essential to our human prosecutorial discretion?

Before we can say what to automate, we have to say what a decision is, and which decisions are constitutive of being a prosecutor at all.

  • Does the choice of emphasis in a charging document count as a decision? Does selecting trial strategy? Does the judgment that a case is "strong"?
  • What are the quintessential decisions that, if AI made them, would nullify the role of a prosecutor?
  • Does the answer change with the size of the consequence: a continuance motion versus a charging decision versus a plea offer?
  • Is the line drawn by the kind of decision, the weight of the decision, or the discretion the decision requires?

The framing — Aristotle on phronesis (practical wisdom) holds that some judgments cannot be reduced to rule-following: law speaks in universals, and fair judgment must correct it “where it is defective owing to its universality” (Nicomachean Ethics V.10, on equity, which NE VI.11 ties to good judgment); Kant called it “self-incurred tutelage” to let “a book which understands for me” stand in for one's own understanding (What is Enlightenment?, 1784, in the English translation at the linked page); Bernard Williams argued that reducing a person's projects and commitments to inputs in an impersonal calculation alienates the actor from his own actions (Utilitarianism: For and Against, 1973); we extend the point: handing a constitutive judgment to a model risks the same estrangement. Authenticity & authorship

What does a person’s signature on an AI-generated statement mean?

A witness gives a halting account; a tool hands back a clean, ordered statement they sign, smoother than anything they actually said. A signed statement is not sworn testimony, but it can later be used to test what the witness says at trial. What happens when the words on the page are not the words the witness chose?

  • The silence problem. If AI subtly tightens "I think it was him" into "It was him", and the witness signs without flagging the change, is that the witness's misstatement or the machine's change? Did the AI change what the witness said? Is silence the same as authorship?
  • Two workflows, two records. In one, the witness dictates, AI cleans up, and every line in the summary links back to the minute and second of the original recording. Accountability holds; every cleaned phrase can be checked against what was said. In the other, the witness reads what AI wrote, signs it, and the original dictation is gone. Same tool, different record.
  • The omitted phrase. Consider the inverse: an AI summary that buries an exculpatory phrase the witness offered. The witness gave it. The signed statement omits it. Who checks for that?
  • The chain of custody parallel. We authenticate a photograph before it is evidence: chain of custody, original file, expert testimony. How is an AI-cleaned witness statement any different from an AI-enhanced photograph?
  • Intent or ratification? Has the signer formed the words they signed, or merely ratified language whose meaning lived somewhere else?
The framing: the federal Jencks Act (18 U.S.C. § 3500(e); many states have analogous rules), under which both a statement the witness “signed or otherwise adopted or approved” and a “substantially verbatim” recording of what she said are producible statements, so discarding the original dictation is not neutral; FRE 901, which requires evidence that an item “is what the proponent claims it is”; Bernard Williams on the two virtues of truth, accuracy and sincerity (Truth and Truthfulness, 2002): a witness can sign sincerely and still not be accurate; and John Searle on assertion: a statement is sincere only if the speaker believes it (Speech Acts, 1969). Applied here, the rewritten text is not an assertion by anyone until the witness adopts it by signing.
Disclosure & Brady

Is it a Brady violation if an AI summary drops exculpatory info and the witness signs it?

Brady reaches not only what the prosecutor knows, but what others on the government's behalf know. AI now sits between the raw evidence and the prosecutor's view of it. The duty is the same whether or not a tool is used.

  • Did we "know"? If an AI summary of a 600-page file dropped the one paragraph that helped the defense, and no human read the original, the duty to learn of favorable evidence known to the prosecution team still attached. A summary that left it out does not excuse the office.
  • Systematic blind spots. If a summarization tool is documented to drop more from non-English speakers, that gap may be exculpatory in every case it touched. One disclosure, or thousands?
  • The early-pointer problem. If AI helped identify a suspect or shaped a charging theory, that output is an early input to the case. The defense may seek the prompt, the output, the confidence score, and the prior version through discovery or a subpoena; whether a court orders production depends on the jurisdiction's rules and the item's relevance. Are we keeping any of them?
The framing — Brady v. Maryland (1963) on the duty to disclose exculpatory evidence; Giglio v. United States (1972) extending the duty to impeachment material; Kyles v. Whitley (1995) imposing on the prosecutor an affirmative duty to learn of favorable evidence known to others acting on the government's behalf, including the police — a duty we read to reach material held in the team's own systems, AI outputs included, though no court has yet said so; and Connick v. Thompson (2011), which held that a single Brady violation does not make a DA's office liable under § 1983 for failure to train; civil liability ordinarily requires a pattern of similar violations; a tool that repeats the same omission across cases could supply one. Sixty-plus years of doctrine now meets a new layer between the evidence and the prosecutor's knowledge of it.
Automation Deskilling

When staff click accept all day, how does the office keep each approval a real check?

Endoscopists who began using AI to help spot precancerous growths during colonoscopy later found fewer of them when working without it: 28.4% before the AI arrived, 22.4% in the months after. The study is observational, but it is consistent with deskilling, in which skill declines when a tool does the noticing.

  • Skill depends on practice. The more we defer to the tool, the less practiced our own judgment becomes, and the less able we are to catch the tool when it is wrong.
  • "Accept, accept, accept." When the suggestion is usually right, checking it starts to feel like wasted time, until the one case where it matters most.
  • Judgment is the skill the office is paid for. Supervising a tool depends on it.
  • Our response. Keep the source on screen (Document Extract) and audit a sample of approvals (Mitigate).
The framing — Parasuraman & Manzey, Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration (Human Factors, 2010) on the canonical mechanism: humans defer to the automated answer and stop looking. Consistent with this, an observational 2025 Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology study found unaided adenoma detection by 19 endoscopists fell from 28.4% to 22.4% after AI was introduced (open summary). Where people defer long enough, the tool's answer can become the only one they see.
Case triage

Can AI triage cases for strength or conviction integrity review?

Triage looks like pure efficiency. But the definition of "strong" is a policy choice. If we never wrote it down, the model wrote it for us — and the cases it does not flag are the part of the policy no one sees.

  • Whose definition. Strong = convictable at trial? Resource-cheapest? Most prosecutable? Each of those directs discretion somewhere different. The definition is a policy choice; the human writes it, or the model does.
  • The unflagged case. A case a careful prosecutor would have prioritized but the model passed over is still a case we owe attention to. A model that stays silent on a case still shapes the outcome, so the office needs a way to see which cases were not flagged.
  • Attention over time. Once "strong" cases get the time, the "weak" ones can stop getting the hard thinking that would have made them stronger. Triage can compound.
The framing — the triage label is not neutral: under Brady v. Maryland (1963) and Kyles v. Whitley (1995), if a tool labels a pending case "weak" and the office proceeds, the label may be information favorable to the accused. Whether an internal assessment like that must be disclosed is contested. Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(a)(2) and its state analogs generally exclude internal work product from discovery, but a work-product label does not override Brady, which reaches material favorable evidence. Before a plea the constitutional duty is also narrower: United States v. Ruiz (2002) held that impeachment material need not be disclosed before a plea agreement, and whether that rule reaches exculpatory evidence is unsettled. After conviction, disclosure duties come mainly from statute, court rule, and ethics rules such as Model Rule 3.8(g)–(h), where a state has adopted them; District Attorney’s Office v. Osborne (2009) declined to recognize a constitutional right to postconviction access to evidence for DNA testing.
The invisible hand

If AI's output preceded your decision, how would you know it didn't shape it?

A tool can hand you a fact. It can also frame what you do next without seeming to. The same model output can serve as a reference or shape the answer — sometimes both, and you cannot always tell which from the inside.

  • The time dimension. If AI identifies a likely suspect before the arrest, that is not the same as AI evaluating the likelihood of correctness after the arrest. One shapes the decision; the other audits it. Same model, same output — opposite role.
  • Even "neutral" output anchors. A case-strength score shown only for reference still shapes the plea offer, the witness order, the closing argument. Decades of anchoring research show that people adjust from the first number they see, and adjust too little; experienced legal professionals showed the effect in the study cited below.
  • The counterfactual test. Would the prosecutor have reached the same decision without seeing the AI output? In practice, without a record of the order of events, the office usually cannot know.
  • Friction is policy. If accepting the AI suggestion is one click and rejecting it requires a memo, the system has already chosen. The same output, with the friction reversed, is a different tool.
  • The disclosure question. If AI materially influenced a charging or plea decision, what do we owe the defense about that influence? Does the model's input belong on the disclosure log? Does Giglio material on a testifying analyst cover that analyst's use of AI?
  • The hidden middle. "AI is only support" is a claim the office should test. The audit trail can show that the AI output preceded the decision. Comparing decisions made with and without the output shows whether it influenced them.
The framing — Tversky & Kahneman, Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases (Science, 1974) on anchoring: people adjust from an initial number, and adjust too little; Englich, Mussweiler & Strack, Playing Dice with Criminal Sentences (Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2006) found the same in experienced legal professionals' sentencing decisions, even when they rolled the anchor themselves with dice; Thaler & Sunstein, Libertarian Paternalism (American Economic Review, 2003) on choice architecture — the default is the policy; and Brady v. Maryland (1963) on the disclosure consequence: the duty would reach a model's output that was favorable to the defense and material, and a defense subpoena under Fed. R. Crim. P. 17(c), which reaches “data,” may also seek it, subject to relevance and admissibility.
Outcomes vs. integrity

Should AI ever sentence a person, even if a measure showed it produced fairer outcomes?

Suppose a tool reduced measured racial disparity in sentences compared with a court's current practice. Should it sentence anyway? We argue it should not, for two reasons: the evidence for such a claim is contested, and the role carries duties a tool cannot hold.

  • Some of what we protect is not an outcome. A judge can be appealed from, recused, and removed, and states reasons on the record. A model is none of those. The system protects more than outcomes: it protects a decision a person can challenge.
  • "Less disparity" is contested measurement. Which fairness measure applies is disputed for COMPAS; Discretion at Scale sets out both measures. "Measurably better" is a claim that has to be earned, not assumed.
  • Kentucky's 2011 pretrial reform tested this for bail. It made risk assessment a mandatory part of bail decisions. Stevenson's study found only a small increase in pretrial release, which eroded “as judges returned to their previous habits,” and no effect on racial disparities in pretrial detention once regional trends were accounted for. In that study, the score did not constrain discretion.
  • Some judgments may not be handed off. Even if the numbers were right, the role itself — sentencing a person on behalf of the public — carries an integrity claim that does not depend on the outcome.
The framing — the clash between consequentialism (choose the better measurable outcome) and the view that some judgments may not be handed off, however good the numbers. On one influential account of punishment, a sentence is censure the community addresses to an offender as a responsible agent, which, we infer, requires someone who can answer for it (R.A. Duff, Punishment, Communication, and Community, 2001); Bernard Williams on integrity argued that reducing a person's projects and commitments to inputs in an impersonal calculation alienates the actor from his own actions (Utilitarianism: For and Against, 1973); we extend the point to delegating a judgment to a model. Whether unequal error rates make a tool unfair depends on the fairness measure adopted; see Discretion at Scale.
Adversarial asymmetry

How does the use of AI in the rest of the legal system impact prosecution?

Defense and prosecution are adversarial by design. Defense advocates for the defendant; that adversarial advocacy is part of how justice is served. We carry duties they don't — and the law treats us differently. The same tool faces different constitutional standards depending on who uses it.

  • Justice, not victory. Our duty (Berger) is to see justice done, not to prevail. Any tool we adopt has to be measurable against that standard, not against whatever the other side is using.
  • Disclosure runs one way. Brady and Kyles put an affirmative duty on us to find and disclose what helps the other side — including from the tools we use. Defense carries no parallel constitutional duty to surface their weaknesses for us. That asymmetry sets the minimum we must meet.
  • Testimonial statements need a witness. Under Crawford and the forensic-analyst cases that followed it, testimonial statements offered against the accused generally require a witness the defense can cross-examine. Federal courts of appeals have treated raw machine output as non-testimonial (United States v. Washington, 4th Cir. 2007; United States v. Lamons, 11th Cir. 2008), and the Supreme Court has left the question open (Bullcoming, Sotomayor, J., concurring). The authentication and expert-testimony rules usually put a person on the stand to sponsor an analysis. How the Confrontation Clause applies to AI-generated analysis is unsettled. The Confrontation Clause protects the accused and places no equivalent requirement on a defending tool.
  • How does the office respond without matching escalation? If we react only by matching what defense uses, we risk losing sight of the asymmetric duties that define us as prosecutors. The conversation has to include defense, the court, and police — not be driven by them.
The framing — Berger v. United States (1935): the prosecutor's interest "is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done" — a duty no defense lawyer carries. Brady v. Maryland (1963) makes the asymmetry concrete: the prosecution must hand over the exculpatory; the defense has no parallel duty to disclose what helps the prosecution (reciprocal discovery aside). Crawford v. Washington (2004), with Melendez-Diaz (2009), Bullcoming (2011) and Smith v. Arizona (2024), requires that testimonial evidence against the accused be open to cross-examination. The same tool, used to accuse versus used to defend, faces different constitutional standards.
The pro se shift

What happens when pro se filings are drafted by AI?

A self-represented defendant with a chatbot is pro se in a changed sense. The motions, the citations, and the arguments may now come from a model the court cannot subpoena and the defendant may not be able to explain. The doctrine assumes a human at the table.

  • The hallucination floor. The fabricated-citation problem seen in civil and appellate courts (see Whiting, below) can reach criminal dockets in filings by defendants who trusted the tool.
  • The leniency convention. Courts read pro se filings liberally because the filer lacks training. Does that liberality still apply when the filing was drafted by an AI tool? Where does the indulgence end and the standard begin?
  • Our response. Do we move to strike on hallucinated authority? Do we flag it for the court? Do we treat AI-assisted pro se filings as represented filings for sanctions purposes? Berger says our duty is justice, not victory; the office should decide in advance how it will respond when the other side's filing comes from a model.
  • The access argument. An indigent defendant facing jail time is entitled to appointed counsel, so a criminal defendant who proceeds pro se has usually waived that right. The access question is sharpest in civil matters and post-conviction filings, where no such right exists. For those filers a chatbot may be the only available way to file. Refusing to engage with AI-assisted pleadings may close that route. Engaging on equal footing may erode the standard.
The framing — Haines v. Kerner (1972) requires courts to construe pro se pleadings liberally because the filer lacks legal training; Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) guarantees appointed counsel to indigent defendants, a right later extended to any case ending in imprisonment (Argersinger v. Hamlin, 1972); there is generally no such right in civil cases, and none in collateral post-conviction review (Pennsylvania v. Finley, 1987). FRCP 11 sanctions extend to pro se litigants in civil cases, though courts have historically applied a softer standard; the Civil Rules do not govern a criminal defendant's filings. The AI-assisted pro se litigant sits between two doctrines that were developed separately.

More Cases Worth Knowing.

What's at stake: the public trust that empowers our offices, the role of human decisions, and the institution itself.

American public prosecution has existed for nearly four hundred years.† If this work is to stay with people, offices will need to say which decisions those are.

Most cases below are civil matters rather than prosecutions; State v. Loomis is a criminal sentencing case. The mechanics carry over. Jul 2016 · Wisc. Sup. Ct. · The first algorithmic warning State v. Loomis & COMPAS: a proprietary algorithm became a sentencing input.

Eric Loomis was sentenced to six years partly on the basis of a COMPAS algorithmic risk-assessment score predicting his likelihood of reoffending. He challenged on due process grounds: the algorithm was a trade secret, he could not audit it, and the math was inaccessible to court, counsel, and defendant alike. The Wisconsin Supreme Court upheld the sentence, noting that the circuit court used COMPAS to corroborate a sentence it would have imposed anyway, and required that presentence reports using COMPAS carry a written advisement to the sentencing court about the tool's limitations. Separately, two months earlier, ProPublica analyzed more than 7,000 COMPAS scores from Broward County, Florida, a pretrial setting, and found unequal error rates by race. Discretion at Scale gives the figures and the vendor's response.

Loomis is an early case on algorithms in our field. The court weighed a proprietary risk score whose method it could not inspect.

Sources: State v. Loomis, 2016 WI 68, 881 N.W.2d 749 (summary); ProPublica, "Machine Bias" (May 2016)

Jun 2023 · S.D.N.Y. · The original Mata v. Avianca: $5,000 sanction. Not knowing AI could invent cases did not excuse standing by them.

Two attorneys at Levidow, Levidow & Oberman filed a federal brief in a routine personal-injury case citing six ChatGPT-generated court decisions that did not exist. When the airline's counsel could not locate the cases, attorney Steven Schwartz returned to ChatGPT and asked if they were real. ChatGPT assured him they were and produced fake excerpts. Judge Castel imposed $5,000 in sanctions, ordered them to notify each judge falsely named as the author of a fake opinion (declining to compel an apology), and held a sanctions hearing. Schwartz testified he was "operating under the false perception that this website could not possibly be fabricating cases on its own."

The defense "I trusted the AI" was rejected in 2023 and is still rejected. The signing attorney owns every citation regardless of which model produced it or who on staff retrieved it.

Source: Mata v. Avianca, Inc., No. 22-cv-1461 (S.D.N.Y. June 22, 2023); LawSites (June 2023)

Jan 2024 · 2d Cir. · Same lawyer, twice Park v. Kim: disciplinary referral. Weeks later a court flagged the same problem in her own pro se case.

Attorney Jae S. Lee filed a Second Circuit reply brief in a medical-malpractice appeal citing Bourguignon v. Coordinated Behavioral Health Services, a case that did not exist. She told the court she had "encountered difficulties" with traditional research tools and asked ChatGPT for help. She did not read or otherwise confirm the suggested case. The court referred her to its Grievance Panel. Weeks later, in her own pro se discrimination action against Delta Air Lines, the district court said at least one case she cited appeared not to exist and may have been generated by AI (Lee v. Delta Air Lines, 2024 WL 1230263 (E.D.N.Y. Mar. 22, 2024)).

The first matter was a referral, and the second was a repeat. An enforced office policy does not rely on each lawyer's good intentions.

Sources: Bloomberg Law (Jan 2024); Lee v. Delta Air Lines (E.D.N.Y. Mar. 22, 2024), Internet Cases summary

Feb 2025 · D. Wyoming · Not only ChatGPT Wadsworth v. Walmart: $5,000 in combined sanctions for hallucinations from the firm's in-house AI tool.

Three attorneys at Morgan & Morgan and the Goody Law Group filed motions in a personal-injury case against Walmart citing eight cases that did not exist. The cases were generated not by ChatGPT but by Morgan & Morgan's in-house AI research tool. Judge Kelly Rankin fined the lead attorney $3,000, revoked his pro hac vice admission, and fined the supervising partner and local counsel $1,000 each.

The vendor's name does not shift responsibility. Enterprise platforms, firm-trained tools, and a firm's own custom assistants can all produce fabricated citations. The signing attorney's duty to verify is identical regardless of who built the system.

Source: Wadsworth v. Walmart Inc., No. 2:23-cv-118-KHR (D. Wyo. Feb. 24, 2025); LawFuel

Jul 2025 · N.D. Ala. · A large firm Johnson v. Dunn: sanctions against lawyers at a large national firm over a deposition motion.

A motion for leave to take a deposition, signed by attorneys at a large litigation firm, cited multiple cases that did not exist. The Northern District of Alabama publicly reprimanded the three lawyers, disqualified them from the case, referred them to the bars where they are licensed, and ordered them to share the opinion with clients, opposing counsel and presiding judges. The court noted that the firm's AI policies and training had not prevented the lapse, and that fines and public embarrassment had not deterred others. Resources do not relieve verification duty.

A large firm's AI policies and training did not by themselves prevent the lapse. Verification has to happen at the point of filing.

Source: Esquire Deposition Solutions, August 2025

Sep 2025 · CA Ct. App. · First published California opinion Noland v. Land of the Free: $10,000 sanction. Published precedent in California.

A California employment-law attorney filed a brief in the Court of Appeal containing AI-hallucinated authorities. The court issued California's first published opinion sanctioning AI hallucinations in court filings, fined the attorney $10,000, and made the holding binding precedent across the state.

This is published authority in California, binding on its trial courts, in the same state where, in January 2026, the California Supreme Court ordered the Court of Appeal to consider sanctions against the Nevada County District Attorney's office. This is a civil standard; how it applies in criminal matters is still developing.

Source: Noland v. Land of the Free, L.P. (Cal. Ct. App. Sept. 12, 2025); McGuireWoods (Sept 2025)

Mar 2026 · 6th Cir. · What the high end looks like Whiting v. City of Athens: $30,000 + full fee shifting against attorneys at the appellate level.

Two Tennessee attorneys filed three consolidated appellate briefs at the Sixth Circuit containing more than two dozen fabricated or misrepresented case citations. A three-judge panel imposed $15,000 in punitive sanctions per attorney, required full reimbursement of the opposing party's fees on appeal plus double costs, and referred both attorneys to the circuit's chief judge for possible discipline. The court did not find that AI produced the errors, and held that no citation, “whether provided by generative AI or any other source,” may go unverified.

These are among the larger sanctions we found: appellate-level sanctions, five-figure fines, and full fee reimbursement. The panel's rule applies whatever the source: every citation must be verified.

Source: Whiting v. City of Athens (6th Cir. Mar. 13, 2026); LawSites (Mar 2026)

Three Responses Any Office Can Use

1 · Review First
Race-Blind First Review

Record a preliminary decision on a redacted report, then review the full file. Log any change with a reason.

At least one state requires this step (California Penal Code § 741, effective January 1, 2025). In the Chohlas-Wood et al. pilot there was “little evidence of disparate treatment” before deployment, so the tool “did not substantially alter charging rates”; the authors do not read this as proof that discrimination is absent.

2 · Move the Decision
A Written Office Rule

A human-authored, versioned rule replaces a model’s weights. The same facts give the same result.

The design standard in Diversion Review v2.x: people can inspect and contest a written rule.

3 · Measure
Consistency Checks

Define like cases in advance (charge, record, evidence). Flag like cases treated unlike, with the uncertainty of each count.

Audit a sample at office level. Use findings to fix the process; a measure used to rank staff becomes a worse measure (Goodhart's law).

What This Does Not Solve

  • A rule keyed on prior record uses a lawful, often required factor, but it can carry forward any disparities in past arrests and convictions.
  • Extraction errors may vary with report style, dialect, or language. Measure accuracy by subgroup.
  • Section 741 lets agencies exclude classes such as domestic violence and sex crimes from blind review.
  • Police and reporting decisions sit upstream; the office can measure what it receives and does.
  • Small offices produce small counts. State the uncertainty.
  • Discovery on a selective-prosecution claim requires “some evidence tending to show” both discriminatory effect and discriminatory intent (United States v. Armstrong, 517 U.S. 456 (1996)). Records of the like-case definition and the follow-up taken may be sought in such discovery, and can support or undercut the claim.

The compounding loop · Problem

Summary Drift.

More of what arrives in the office may be AI-written: police reports, vendor summaries, prior filings. Our own AI tools then summarize that output, and the next tool summarizes the summary. Each pass is a chance to drift, and errors can compound rather than cancel out.

AI research has a name for a related effect, model collapse: Shumailov et al. (Nature, 2024) showed that models trained on other models' output lose the rare cases first. Summary drift is an analogous loss inside one record, as each summary feeds the next.

SourceA human writes the original report. One ambiguous phrase: "found near the vehicle."
→
AI summarizesA tool tightens it to "found in the vehicle." The phrase reads as more certain, and it is wrong.
→
AI on AIThe next tool summarizes that summary, then the next. The hedge is gone, and the error reads as a statement of fact.

Each pass feeds the next · rare and uncertain details are dropped first · distortion can compound rather than average out

Why it happens

  • AI output tends to read as confident. Hedges and rare details are often smoothed away first.
  • As more of what comes into the office is AI-written, our tools are increasingly summarizing other AI, not a human record.
  • Every summarization step is lossy. Across three steps, the losses can compound and are hard to see.

Why it matters for us

  • A "near the vehicle" that becomes "in the vehicle" can be the difference between a charge that holds and one that doesn't.
  • We may never see the original once three layers of summary sit on top of it.
  • The error arrives looking polished and certain, which makes it hard to catch.
The compounding loop, continued

Mitigate.

The prosecutor signs the document and answers for it in court. So the prosecutor sets the terms. For any AI-generated content in the office, demand:

  1. The raw source travels with the output. Every AI-generated piece arrives with the prompts that produced it and the evidence and primary sources it drew on. We do not recommend relying on AI output in a filing without its source.
  2. Disclosed failure history. The vendor tells you where the AI in their product has already caused legal problems. Treat a refusal to answer as a risk.
  3. A complete authorship audit log. A full record of which content the AI generated and which a human wrote or edited, with timestamps and version history.
  4. Visibility into whether review still happens. A sampled audit shows whether verification is still taking place, so the office can fix the workflow. It is not used to rank individuals.
Systemic AI

Systemic AI

Every case involves multiple actors. Each will use AI differently. Understanding the risk each actor poses — to evidence, to proceedings, to your case — is part of the job.

Prosecutors

Prosecutors decide whether charges should be filed and what outcomes to pursue. Unlike every other advocate, prosecutors have an obligation to seek justice, not simply to win.
AI Uses
  • Discovery review
  • Report summarization
  • Evidence organization
  • Trial preparation
  • Motion drafting
  • Identifying Brady material
  • Flagging inconsistent witness statements
Police usually start a case; the prosecutor decides whether it goes forward and on what charge. A mistake at that decision affects every stage that follows.
Example · Brady Miss

Prosecutor uploads 10,000 pages of discovery. AI summarizes to ten pages.

One favorable witness statement never appears in the summary.

Disclosure risk. The human trusted the summary instead of reviewing the original. Whether this becomes a Brady violation depends on suppression and materiality; the duty to find the statement still attached.

Example · Charge Suggestion

A future system recommends: Charge Aggravated Assault.

If nobody independently evaluates the recommendation, the charging decision has in effect been delegated to a machine.

Ethical Concerns
  • Charging decisions must remain human decisions?Open questionIf a model suggests the charge and a human signs it, at what point is the charging decision delegated to the model?
  • The disclosure determination cannot be delegated; the review process can be, under supervision
  • AI may identify issues but should not make final decisions

Defense

Defense attorneys protect the rights of the accused and force the government to prove its case. Their ethical duties differ from prosecutors because they respond to state power rather than exercise it.
AI Uses
  • Discovery review
  • Brady searches
  • Prior testimony review
  • Motion drafting
  • Cross-examination preparation
  • Legal research
Defense AI will often be used to find mistakes in prosecution work. The adversarial system is designed for that. The concern is volume, reliability, and the burden that high-volume AI-assisted filings place on courts.
Example · Discovery at Scale

Prosecutor produces 50,000 pages. Defense AI reviews every page.

It surfaces inconsistent officer statements, conflicting witness accounts, Brady material, and impeachment evidence.

Prosecutors may be challenged on issues that earlier review might not have found.

Example · High-Volume Motions

Previously: five motions filed. Now: twenty. All require a response.

The cost of filing fell. The cost of responding did not.

Ethical Concerns
  • Confidential client information in public AI systems
  • Filing hallucinated cases or citations
  • Overreliance on AI legal analysis

Pro Se

A pro se defendant represents themselves without an attorney.
AI Uses
  • Legal research
  • Motion drafting
  • Trial preparation
  • Plea evaluation
  • Understanding procedures
Access gap: A defendant released pretrial can use AI freely. A defendant held in jail may have no access at all. If AI becomes a meaningful legal tool, detention creates an additional disadvantage.
Example · Wrong Advice on Plea

Defendant: Should I take this plea deal?

AI: You have a strong case.

Defendant rejects a favorable offer. At trial they are convicted and receive a significantly harsher sentence.

AI had no information about the local judge, witnesses, jury pool, or practical realities of trial.

Ethical Concerns
  • AI-generated false alibis or fake supporting documents
  • Fabricated legal citations

Police

Police generate the initial reports that prosecutors rely upon. They sit upstream.
AI Uses
  • Report drafting
  • Body camera summarization
  • Interview transcription
  • Evidence organization
An error in a police report can carry into charging, trial, and appeal. Once the file is closed, the source of the error may not be recoverable. For disclosure purposes, police are part of the prosecution team (What the Office Knows). Ask each agency: does your report tool keep every draft?
Example · Report Changes the Facts

Officer: the gun was near the vehicle.

AI report: the gun was in the vehicle.

Nobody notices. The charge is built on an error introduced by AI.

Example · No Audit Trail

AI creates the initial draft. The draft is deleted. Only the final version remains.

Months later: nobody can determine what the officer wrote, what the AI wrote, or what changes were made.

Ethical Concerns
  • Signing reports without verification
  • AI altering facts without a record
  • Missing audit trails
  • Inability to explain report generation

Victims

Victims are people harmed by crime. Every state recognizes victims' rights, many in its constitution, and in federal cases the Crime Victims' Rights Act grants rights to notice and to be heard (18 U.S.C. § 3771). The charging decision rests with the office.
AI Uses
  • Support chatbots
  • Translation
  • Impact statement drafting
  • Resource navigation
The office should hear the victim's actual position, not a machine-generated version of it.
Example · AI Writes the Impact Statement

A victim provides a few notes. AI generates a detailed statement.

The final statement contains language the victim never actually used.

The court hears a machine-generated voice presented as the victim's own.

Example · Unverified Evidence of Harm

A victim uploads photographs. The images appear authentic.

Later: review finds they were generated or modified by AI. The case file then contains evidence that was not authentic.

Ethical Concerns
  • Authenticity and consent
  • Unverified or altered evidence
  • Misrepresentation of victim's actual wishes

Witnesses

Witnesses tell the court what they observed.
AI Uses
  • Statement review
  • Interview summarization
  • Preparation assistance
A statement cleaned by AI can be challenged on cross-examination. An AI-built record can affect whether a witness is believed before they testify.
Example · AI Coaches the Witness

Witness uploads a prior statement: What should I say if they ask about inconsistencies?

AI generates answers. The witness studies them before trial.

At trial: actual memory and AI-generated explanations are indistinguishable.

Example · AI Generates Supporting Evidence

Witness creates AI-generated receipts, screenshots, and texts to support their account.

The evidence appears authentic. Its origin may not surface until late in the case.

Ethical Concerns
  • Witness coaching and memory contamination
  • Evidence of uncertain authenticity
  • Risk of inaccurate testimony

Experts

Experts explain technical subjects to judges and juries.
AI Uses
  • Data analysis
  • Pattern detection
  • Report drafting
  • Forensic assistance
Under FRE 702, the party offering expert testimony must show it is “the product of reliable principles and methods.” An expert who relies on a black box system may not be able to make that showing.
Example · Black Box Methodology

Defense: How did the software reach this result?

Expert: I don't know.

The expert cannot explain the methodology. The testimony is vulnerable to challenge on admissibility.

Example · Prior Testimony at Scale

AI reviews hundreds of prior expert transcripts.

Defense identifies statements from years ago that contradict today's testimony.

The expert's credibility is challenged using their own record — at a scale previously impossible.

Ethical Concerns
  • Unknown error rates in automated systems
  • Black box methodology — cannot be explained or tested?Open questionIf an expert relies on an AI tool whose method cannot be explained, can the opinion meet the admissibility standard for reliable methodology?
  • Overreliance on automated conclusions

Judges

Judges make legal decisions and ensure proceedings are fair.
AI Uses
  • Research assistance
  • Opinion drafting
  • Record summarization
If the bench reads an AI summary of a motion, the attorney's argument is filtered before it is weighed. Neither party knows. Neither party can challenge it.
Example · Motion Summary Misses the Point

Judge receives a 60-page motion. AI generates a one-page summary.

The summary misses the strongest argument.

Neither party knows. Neither party can challenge it.

Example · Detention Recommendation

System recommends detention. A judge accepts it when it matches the judge's own view and sets it aside when it does not.

Whether that raises or lowers disparity is an empirical question; see the Kentucky study in Our Conversation.

  • Hidden influences on judicial decisions
  • Accountability and transparency
  • Reliance on black box recommendations?Open questionIf a judge adopts an algorithmic detention recommendation whose reasoning cannot be inspected, how can either party contest it on the record?

Jurors

Jurors determine guilt or innocence based solely on evidence admitted in court.
Improper AI Uses
  • Summarizing testimony during deliberations
  • Researching legal issues outside court
  • Asking AI whether the evidence is sufficient to convict
Every other actor may use AI to prepare. Jurors must decide cases based only on evidence presented in court. There is no exception.
Example · Juror Asks ChatGPT

Juror: Is this enough evidence to convict?

The AI answers. The response enters deliberations.

Neither side knows it happened. Neither side can challenge the information.

Example · Juror Researches Forensics

A juror does not understand DNA evidence. Instead of asking through the court, they ask AI.

The juror receives information that was never admitted at trial.

There is no legitimate role for AI in jury deliberations.

Appellate

Appellate attorneys review the full trial record for reversible error. Every discovery failure, improper statement, and Brady miss the trial produced is now their raw material.
AI Uses
  • Full trial transcript review
  • Brief drafting and legal research
  • Precedent search at scale
  • Identifying preserved vs. waived errors
  • Pattern detection across prior convictions
  • Habeas corpus petition drafting
The appellate record is a full-text database subject to AI mining. Every line of transcript, every production log, every voir dire exchange is searchable in minutes. The trial record was always the record — it just couldn't be read this fast.
Example · Record Review at Scale

Defense appellate AI reviews 3,000 pages of trial transcripts overnight.

It flags a Brady disclosure buried on page 2,847 of a late production, a Batson pattern across three days of voir dire, and two instances of improper bolstering.

The appellate court now has a list of issues trial counsel did not raise.

Example · Pattern Detection Across Cases

AI reviews 200 prior convictions from the same office over five years.

The same detective's "plain smell" stop language appears in 87 cases, nearly verbatim.

A pattern invisible across individual case files becomes visible at scale.

Ethical Concerns
  • Hallucinated case citations filed in appellate briefs, which courts have sanctioned
  • AI may surface possible Brady issues in the record, which can support a claim on appeal
  • Habeas petitions drafted by AI indistinguishable from lawyer-crafted ones

Design standard

Traceability or Disable the Feature

If a model writes it, a reader should be able to click it and see where it came from. The design standard: every AI-generated sentence carries a link back to the primary source, one hover or tap from the original document.

  • Sentence-level, not document-level. "It's in the file somewhere" is not traceability. The link lands on the line.

  • It survives the chain. Even after layers of summary, the trail back to the human-authored original stays intact.
  • Ask your vendor for it by name. See the vendor questions: "If I highlight a description the AI wrote, can I open the exact document it was pulled from?"
What the office can show

What the Office Knows.

The disclosure duty reaches material in the prosecution’s file for the case, whether or not a lawyer has read it. AI changes how much an office can find, and what its records show about what it found.

Three Layers of Knowledge

1 · In the Case File · Supreme Court
Attributed Knowledge

Evidence “highly probative of innocence” in the prosecutor’s file is presumed recognized “even if he has actually overlooked it.” Agurs, 427 U.S. at 110. The office is “an entity.” Giglio, 405 U.S. at 154.

2 · Cheap to Find · Circuit Law
The Duty to Search

“As the burden of the proposed examination rises, clearly the likelihood of a pay-off must also rise before the government can be put to the effort.” Brooks, D.C. Cir. A search that missed a record was “merely a token effort.” Perdomo, 3d Cir.

Our reading: cheaper search may lower that threshold and widen what a court expects the office to have found.

3 · Surfaced to a Person · Ethics Rule, Our Reading
Actual Knowledge

ABA Op. 09-454 reads the rule’s “knowledge” as “actual knowledge,” which “may be inferred from [the] circumstances.” A flag to a named person is such a circumstance. The rule creates no duty to investigate.

A dismissed flag can show the office saw the item; it does not by itself show the office knew the item was favorable. Log the reason.

Settled and Open

Settled
  • Good or bad faith does not matter. Brady, 373 U.S. at 87.
  • The prosecutor must learn of favorable evidence known to the prosecution team. Kyles, 514 U.S. at 437.
Open
  • Whether evidence the office possesses but no one knows of is “suppressed.” Hartline et al. (2026) argue it is.
  • Whether a tool’s configuration is itself discoverable.
  • Whether an office can know what no member has read. Lackey (2021) ties group knowledge to what members believe and have grounds for; Bird (2014) argues a group can know what no member does. Attributed knowledge resembles Bird’s view.
  • We did not find an appellate decision on AI-assisted disclosure review in our research as of October 2026.

Federal guidance only: Justice Manual 9-5.002 permits “computerized searches,” and adds: “prosecutors should not delegate the disclosure determination itself.”

An Epistemic Chain of Custody

An epistemic chain of custody records what the office’s tools did with each item.

1 · Ingested
What Entered

Source, date, and a content hash.

2 · Processed
What Each Pass Read

Pages covered, failures, model and settings version.

3 · Surfaced
What Was Flagged

To whom, when, any AI output shown before the decision, and the decision with a reason.

4 · Filtered
What Was Ranked Low

Exclusions, with a separate pass for favorable material.

Summaries should link to source pages (traceability); a disclosure determination rests on the record itself. Internal logs may be work product (Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(a)(2) and state analogs), which does not excuse a Brady disclosure. Banks calls “prosecutor may hide, defendant must seek” “not tenable” (540 U.S. at 696); a log can show what the office looked at and when.

Why the Disclosure Determination Stays With a Person

The disclosure determination is a constitutive judgment (Ship of Theseus). Tools find, rank, and log. The prosecutor decides. The determination should not be delegated (see ABA Op. 512); the review work can be delegated, under supervision. A prosecutor who doubts a flag routes it to a supervisor, and the log records who decided and why. Following the process bears on professional responsibility; it does not change whether the item had to be disclosed.

Rate a Feature · Methodology

How to Rate an AI Feature

The question is how the tool is built, not whether it uses AI. The ratings describe designs, not any vendor's shipped product. Tasks are labeled in The Ship of Theseus; builds are rated here.

The two axes

  • Value — realistic time saved × how often you would use it. Not the marketing pitch.
  • Risk — harm if the output is wrong × probability the output is wrong, including rare errors with costly results.

The four verdicts

  • Don'tThe build cannot be made safe by configuration. Reject regardless of vendor claims.
  • PilotThe build could be safe with specific safeguards. Run it with logging and sampled review before widening.
  • AdoptThe build is sound by design. Operate it and monitor.
  • TrickNo need for AI; the simpler solution wins.

The Recurring Shape of an Adopt

AI extracts named facts from the source. The office writes a rule that turns those facts into an outcome. The same facts always produce the same outcome. Anyone can read the rule, and it only changes when the office changes it. We call this a data-based policy.

Diversion Auto-Flag

TaskDecide which incoming cases the diversion coordinator should review for eligibility. The same builds appear in the worked example, Diversion Review.

v1.0 AI Opinion Don't Value High · Risk High

What this build does

AI reads each incident report and outputs yes or no. The criteria live in a prompt no attorney reviewed. The answer cites no specific fact in the report.

Pros

  • Surfaces cases an at-capacity coordinator might miss.
  • Fast — no human bottleneck.

Cons

  • No record of why any specific case was excluded.
  • Rewording the prompt changes who gets flagged.
  • Disparate flagging is hard to trace to a cause from outside the model.
Why Don't:

Discretion is delegated to an opaque model with no audit trail.

v1.1 + Reasoning Don't Value High · Risk High

What this build does

Same yes/no as v1.0, plus a 1-3 sentence rationale generated after the verdict.

Pros

  • Feels transparent — rationale is something to point at.
  • Slightly easier to spot wild misses.

Cons

  • Rationale is generated after the verdict — post-hoc justification, not reasoning.
  • Two similar cases get opposite calls with equally careful-sounding text.
  • Audit-by-reading the rationale habituates trust in the model's voice.
Why Don't:

The rationale rationalizes a conclusion the model already reached. The model is still doing the discretion.

v2.0 Extract → Policy Adopt Value High · Risk Med

What this build does

AI extracts a fixed list of facts from the report (each cited to a paragraph). The office writes a deterministic rule against those facts: IF violence=none AND priors=0 AND offense=property AND restitution=paid THEN flag=diversion-review.

Pros

  • Identical facts → identical flag.
  • Rule is auditable; office can change it deliberately.
  • Each fact traces to a source paragraph.

Cons

  • Extraction errors propagate.
  • Rule has gaps the office didn't anticipate, such as excluding people who cannot pay restitution in full.
  • Needs ongoing sample audit of unflagged cases.
Why Adopt:

The rule, not the model, sets the flag. AI fills facts; the office's written rule does the flagging. Log every extraction; sample-audit unflagged cases.

v2.1 + Pattern Scout Adopt Value High · Risk Low

What this build does

Same fact-extraction + rule as v2.0. In parallel, AI clusters cases the rule did not flag and surfaces recurring fact patterns (e.g. 12 unflagged cases share restitution arranged but not yet complete + first offense). Suggestions queue for office review; nothing auto-applies.

Pros

  • Deterministic rule still gates.
  • AI is purely additive — only proposes.
  • Office decides which patterns become policy.
  • Surfaces edge cases the rule didn't anticipate.

Cons

  • Pattern suggestions can bias the coordinator toward the AI's clustering.
  • New fields must be reviewed for the disparity risks the rule was designed to avoid.
Why Adopt:

The rule is the floor; AI only proposes additions; the office controls what changes.

  • ModelThe trained system that generates text. On its own it predicts the next words.
  • GenAIAI that writes new text in its own voice, such as drafts, summaries, and motions. Its speed comes from skipping the source.
  • ChatbotA conversation interface that answers in natural language. It is usually powered by GenAI underneath: its answers vary with the wording and can lack source citations.
  • AgentA program that takes actions on your behalf: it finds, extracts, organizes, and presents. It returns the source, and a person still reads and decides.
  • InterfaceHow the AI presents itself to you, such as a chat box or a review panel.
  • Host / environmentThe controlled environment where the model runs (e.g., AWS Bedrock, AgentCore), with its own data rules.
  • Model vs. host vs. interfaceThree layers: the model generates text, the host is where it runs, and the interface is what you see. The same model is safer in a controlled host than in a public app.
  • PromptThe instruction or question given to a model. Different wording can produce different answers.
  • Prompt engineeringChoosing the wording and structure of the ask to get a better, more reliable answer.
  • In-context learningGiving the model examples or notes inside the prompt so it adapts on the spot, without retraining.
  • Fine-tuningA short, targeted retraining on your examples so the model does one job better.
  • TrainingThe months-long, expensive process that builds the base model from large amounts of data.
  • System promptCore instructions, usually not shown to the user, that set the model’s baseline behavior and rules.
  • Evaluation (eval)A fixed test set that measures what a model can and cannot do before you rely on it.
  • HallucinationWhen the model states something false with confidence, because it predicts plausible text, not verified fact.
  • TraceabilityBeing able to follow any AI output back to the source document it came from.
  • Human-in-the-loopA review step in which a person checks or approves a system’s output. It is necessary but not sufficient: a person who reviews a result does not by that act become the source of the decision or gain control over it.
  • Human at the helmA way of placing a person in an AI-assisted process. The person sets direction and owns the decision, rather than only checking output after it is produced. Here the helm is named people who can give the decision’s reasons and answer for it: the chief prosecutor (elected or appointed), who sets written policy; supervisors, who review; and the prosecutor who decides the case. Alex Pearson (2026) contrasts the idea with “in the loop.”
  • Meaningful human controlA standard for human control of automated systems, from Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven (2018), which grew out of the debate over autonomous weapons. The system must track the moral reasons of the relevant people, including designers and policymakers, meaning it responds to them. Its outcomes must trace back to a person who understands the system and their own role in it. Having a person nearby is not enough to meet it.
  • Moral crumple zoneMadeleine Elish’s term (2019) for a person held responsible for the outcomes of an automated system they had limited control over. It names a gap between who is held to account and who is in control.
  • Constitutive vs. characteristicA constitutive feature is part of what makes a thing the kind of thing it is; remove it and the thing changes in kind. A characteristic feature is typical of the thing but can change without altering what it is. Sitting at a board is characteristic of chess; following its rules is constitutive. John Searle calls rules that create an activity constitutive, and rules that govern an activity that already exists regulative.
  • SoritesA puzzle about vague terms (from the Greek soros, “heap”). One grain of sand is not a heap, and adding one grain never turns a non-heap into a heap, yet enough grains make one. It shows how steps that are each too small to matter can add up to a real change.
  • Ship of TheseusA thought experiment about identity over time. A ship has its planks replaced one by one until none of the original wood remains. Is it still the same ship, and what would make it so?
  • PhronesisAristotle’s word for practical wisdom: the trained ability to judge what to do in a particular situation. It grows from experience with particular cases, not from rules alone. He treats it as a state of the person who acts, not as information that can be handed to someone else.
  • CJISThe FBI’s Criminal Justice Information Services Division, whose security policy governs criminal justice information. Tools the policy does not cover may not handle that information.
  • Summary driftThe loss of detail and hedging when AI summarizes AI-written material: each pass drops rare and uncertain points, and errors accumulate. It is separate from model drift, a change in a model’s behavior over time. It is related to model collapse, the degradation of models trained on other models’ output, in which rare cases disappear first (Shumailov et al., Nature, 2024).
  • Brady materialEvidence favorable to the accused that the prosecution must disclose to the defense, under Brady v. Maryland (1963); the duty covers what others acting for the government know.
  • Giglio materialImpeachment evidence, which undermines a witness's credibility, that the prosecution must disclose, extending Brady (Giglio v. United States, 1972).
  • ExculpatoryEvidence that tends to clear the accused or reduce culpability; the prosecution must disclose it when it is material.
  • Chain of custodyThe documented, unbroken record of who handled a piece of evidence and when. It is how evidence is authenticated before admission. An epistemic chain of custody applies the idea to knowledge: a record of what the office’s tools did with each item.
  • COMPASA proprietary risk-assessment tool used in criminal cases. ProPublica’s 2016 analysis found that, among defendants who did not reoffend, 44.9% of Black defendants and 23.5% of white defendants were labeled higher risk. The vendor responded that scores were equally accurate across groups by another measure, predictive parity.
  • Pro seA party who represents themselves without an attorney (Latin: “for oneself”). A criminal defendant may waive counsel and proceed this way.
  • Jury deliberationsThe closed discussion in which jurors decide guilt or innocence — by rule, on the admitted evidence alone.
  • Black boxA system whose input and output can be seen but whose reasoning cannot be inspected or explained.
  • DeskillingThe gradual loss of a professional’s own skills after relying on AI for a long period.
  • Attributed (constructive) knowledgeKnowledge the law treats a person or organization as having, whether or not anyone learned it. Evidence in a prosecutor’s case file that is highly probative of innocence is presumed known, and some courts also hold an office to what a reasonable search would have found.
  • Prosecution teamThe prosecutor and the others acting for the government in a case, such as investigating officers. Under Kyles v. Whitley, the prosecutor must learn of favorable evidence known to them.
  • EpistemicConcerning knowledge: what is known, by whom, and how. An epistemic record shows what a person or organization held, what it saw, and when.
  • DiscretionThe freedom to choose among lawful options, such as whether to charge, which charge to file, or whether to divert. The law sets limits, but no rule fixes the choice in advance.
  • Mandatory minimumA statutory floor on the sentence for an offense. Once a charge carrying one is proved, a court generally cannot sentence below it.
  • Error-rate balanceA fairness measure that asks whether a prediction tool makes the same kinds of mistakes at the same rates for each group, for example how often people who did not reoffend were labeled high risk.
  • Predictive parityA fairness measure that asks whether a high-risk label is equally accurate for every group. Of the people labeled high risk, the share who reoffend should match across groups. Northpointe’s 2016 report uses this term.
  • Race-blind first reviewA two-step review in which a prosecutor first decides on a report with race-identifying details removed, then reviews the full file. Any change between the two decisions is recorded with a reason.
  • AnswerableA person is answerable for a judgment when it is open to a demand for their reasons (Shoemaker, 2011). That differs from being held to account, which means being liable to blame or sanction for what was done (Watson, 1996).
  • Design standardWhat a tool should do before an office relies on it, and what Paradigm builds toward and holds vendors to. It is a recommendation, not a claim that a feature exists today and not a professional standard.
  • DisparityA difference in outcomes between groups. It shows where to look, but does not by itself show which decision caused it.
