Ethical AI in Prosecution

A field guide for prosecutors' offices · and the vendors who serve them
00 · The Question

Why Are We Here?

This is the moment that decides what prosecution becomes. We are the ones who lead it. Over the next pages we draw one line, walk five patterns of AI use against it, and end with the questions none of us can settle alone.

01 · The Definition

01We Must Define Ethical AI for Ourselves

What do we mean when we say: “Prosecution cannot be automated”?

Over the past year, AI has appeared in every one of our digital tools, and the pace is accelerating. If each tool automates its own slice of the work, at what point have we outsourced prosecution?

At what point do the people who set our budgets — local, state, federal — decide we need fewer paralegals and fewer attorneys, because of “AI efficiency”? ?

The crux What will we point to as irreplaceable? The rest of this artifact is one attempt at an answer: AI may extract, find, and check; the prosecutor still reads, decides, writes, and signs.
02 · The Slippery Slope

02The Existential Slippery Slope

Each new tool automates one more slice of the work. Alone, each looks harmless. The line we have to hold is between AI that helps us decide and AI that decides for us.

Read the discretion spectrum from L1 toward L5. The further right, the more the tool is making the call a prosecutor is paid to make.

The easy no Charge selection (L5) is the easy no — no prosecutor lets a machine decide whom to charge. The hard calls live between the ends; we score one below.

Pick Any Tool From the Spectrum. Weigh Both Sides.

For every AI feature, weigh what it gives you against what it could cost you. The two are rarely the same size.

Value what you gain
  • When it works as intended, what are we gaining?
  • For the piece being automated, how much time, effort, or overall cost are you really saving?
  • How good is the feature actually at its intended task?
Risk what it could cost
  • Could this tool cause a career-ending mistake for someone in my office?
  • What thinking or decisions is the feature replacing, and what is lost by having an AI do it?
  • When it errs — adding what isn't there (hallucinated citation, wrong tag, phantom field) or missing what is (buried line, unflagged case, skipped field) — what are the consequences, and who bears the weight?
A worked example — is it okay for AI to flag cases for Diversion Review?

Weigh the considerations before you build it:

  • Its silence becomes a denial: if you only look at what it flags, the cases it skips are passed over with no one reviewing them.
  • The opinion can't be audited: you cannot see why one case was flagged and another was not.
  • It can drift past the obvious candidates a human would have caught.
  • The harm runs one way: a wrongful skip is a person who did not get the diversion they qualified for.

We work this all the way through in Appendix II: from a raw AI opinion to an auditable policy with an AI flagger on top.

04 · The Strategy

04Our Actionable Strategy

Prosecutors study human nature for a living. We have seen how desperation distorts judgment, how “justice” gets rationalized, how careful people slip one small choice at a time.

1

Concretely. Concisely. Repeatedly.

2
Protect 02 · Staff

Build technical and procedural safeguards into the tools themselves, so the right choice is the easy one and the wrong one requires effort.
3
Protect 03 · Integrity

For Later · The Open Questions

§Let's Have the Conversation

This is beyond politics. Where the line falls between AI that serves justice and AI that replaces it will not be settled by any one office. A few starters below — tap to open. Each one is an unresolved tension, not a settled answer.

Decision BoundaryWhich decisions, if a machine made them, would end prosecution as a role??+

Before we can say what to automate, we have to say what a decision is, and which decisions are constitutive of being a prosecutor at all.

  • Does the choice of emphasis in a charging document count as a decision? Selecting trial strategy? The judgment that a case is “strong”?
  • What are the quintessential decisions that, if AI made them, would nullify the role of a prosecutor?
  • Does the answer change with the size of the consequence: a continuance motion vs. a charging decision vs. a plea offer?

Try it — label each: routine continuance · summarizing 600 pages of discovery · phrasing an indictment's opening sentence · choosing which witnesses to call · selecting which charges to file · recommending plea terms · assessing a victim's credibility · identifying Brady material · seeking pretrial detention · dismissing after new evidence. Mark each AI OK / AI-assisted / Human only — the boundary you draw is your ethical AI policy. The places you disagree with a colleague are the conversation we have to have.

The framing: Aristotle on phronesis; Kant on autonomy; Bernard Williams on integrity; and ABA Model Rule 5.3 — the supervising attorney remains responsible for work by any non-lawyer assistant, including the model.
AuthorshipWhen AI cleans up a witness's words, who signed for the truth??+

A witness gives a halting account; a tool hands back a clean, ordered statement they sign — smoother than anything they actually said.

  • The silence problem. If AI tightens “I think it was him” into “It was him,” and the witness signs without flagging it, is that the witness's lie or the machine's?
  • Two workflows, two worlds. One links every cleaned line back to the minute and second of the recording. The other discards the original dictation. Same tool. Opposite world.
  • The cutting-room floor. An AI summary that buries an exculpatory phrase the witness offered. Whose duty is it to catch that?
  • The chain of custody parallel. How is an AI-cleaned statement different from an AI-enhanced photograph?
The Jencks Act (18 U.S.C. § 3500) and best-evidence rule (FRE 1002); Walter Benjamin on authenticity; Searle's Chinese Room on intent vs. ratification.
Disclosure & BradyWhat happens when an AI summary drops exculpatory information??+

Brady reaches not only what the prosecutor knows, but what others on the government's behalf know. AI now sits between the raw evidence and the prosecutor's view of it. The duty does not bend to the tool.

  • Did we “know”? If an AI summary dropped the one helpful paragraph and no human read the original, the duty still attached.
  • Systematic blind spots. If a tool is documented to drop more from non-English speakers, that gap may be exculpatory in every case it touched. One disclosure, or thousands?
  • The early-pointer problem. If AI shaped a charging theory, that output is the earliest material decision in the chain. Are we keeping the prompt, output, confidence score, prior version?
Brady v. Maryland (1963); Giglio (1972); Kyles v. Whitley (1995) — an affirmative duty to learn of evidence known to others, including the team's tools; Connick v. Thompson (2011) on office liability for a pattern of failures.
De-SkillingWhen we click “accept” all day, what happens to our own judgment??+

Doctors given AI help reading scans got better at first. After about three months their unaided accuracy slipped — they had quietly stopped looking as hard. Outsource the thinking long enough and the skill erodes (de-skilling).

  • The skill is a muscle. The more we defer, the less able we are to catch the tool when it is wrong.
  • “Accept, accept, accept.” When the suggestion is usually right, checking it feels like wasted time — until the one case where it matters most.
  • The expertise we lose is the expertise we are paid for.
Parasuraman & Manzey, Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation (2010); a 2025 Lancet Gastro & Hep. study found unaided adenoma detection fell from 28.4% to 22.4% after sustained AI exposure (summary).
Case TriageShould AI be able to triage cases by which are strong or weak??+

Sorting the docket by “strength” sounds like pure efficiency. The trouble is what “strong” means, what it crowds out, and what the AI quietly leaves unflagged.

  • Define “strong” first. Convictability at trial? The definition is itself a policy choice.
  • Priority distortion. Do “strong” cases get worked while the hard thinking that would strengthen a “weak” one never happens?
  • The unflagged case. What is the harm when the AI does not flag a case a human would clearly have prioritized?
When a tool labels a case “weak,” the driving facts are often favorable to the accused; Kyles (1995) forecloses the argument that the duty evaporates because the assessment lived in a tool. The evidence underneath is Brady territory.
The Invisible HandIf AI's output preceded your decision, how would you know it didn't shape it??+

A tool can hand you a fact. It can also frame what you do next without seeming to.

  • The time dimension. AI identifying a suspect before arrest is not AI auditing likelihood after. Same model, opposite role.
  • Even “neutral” output anchors. A case-strength score shown only for reference still shapes the plea, the witness order, the closing.
  • Friction is policy. If accepting the suggestion is one click and rejecting requires a memo, the system has already chosen.
  • The hidden middle. We tell ourselves the human decided. How would we know if it influenced?
Tversky & Kahneman (1974) on anchoring; Thaler & Sunstein (2003) — the default is the policy; Brady on the disclosure consequence.
Outcomes vs. IntegrityCould a study ever justify replacing the judge??+

Imagine an AI that sentences with less racial disparity than the judges in your courthouse. Should we let the machine sentence? No.

  • What we protect is not measurable. Discretion, mercy, and a face that can be held accountable are constitutive of the role.
  • “Less disparity” is contested. COMPAS was sold as neutral; ProPublica (2016) showed it misclassified Black defendants as high-risk at ~twice the rate of white defendants.
  • What if AI just recommends to judges? Stevenson (2018) on Kentucky: judges followed the score when it matched their priors and dismissed it when it did not; disparity did not improve. The “human in the loop” filtered for the bias.
Consequentialism vs. the deontological claim; Williams on integrity. The “measurably better” premise has not been earned.
Adversarial AsymmetryDoes the same GenAI tool become ethical in defense hands — but not in ours??+

Defense and prosecution are adversarial by design. But the power is not symmetric.

  • The prosecutor accuses and carries the burden; the defense responds. So “if defense uses GenAI, we can too” does not hold.
  • A chatbot that helps the defense exculpate may serve justice; the same tool in the accusing hand is more dangerous.
  • How do we work with defense to avoid an arms race that erodes the system out of fear of falling behind?
Berger v. United States (1935): the prosecutor's interest “is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done.” Brady makes the asymmetry concrete; Crawford (2004) on confronting testimonial evidence.
The Pro Se ShiftWhat happens when “pro se” becomes synonymous with “AI-represented”??+

A self-represented defendant with a chatbot is no longer pro se in the old sense. The doctrine assumes a human at the table.

  • The hallucination floor. The same fabricated-citation problem that sanctions firms will land in our courts, signed by a defendant who trusted the tool.
  • The leniency convention. Courts read pro se filings liberally. Does that still apply when the filing was drafted by a model?
  • The access argument. For defendants priced out of counsel, a chatbot may be the only door to the courthouse. There is no easy answer.
Haines v. Kerner (1972) on liberal construction; Gideon (1963); FRCP 11 sanctions. The AI-assisted pro se litigant sits between two doctrines never built to talk to each other.

§Four Actions for Your Office

1
Protect 01 · Communities
Communicate Your Policy
Concretely. Concisely. Repeatedly.
Once you have drawn your line, put it in writing where any staff member can find it. Name what is fine (an internal email drafted with Copilot) and what is prohibited (anything filed in court or in the record). Distribute it; refresh it quarterly as the tools change.
2
Protect 02 · Staff
Implement Safeguards
Defaults beat individual judgment under stress.
Build the policy into the tools so the safe choice is the easy one. Block consumer AI (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok) at the network, not on the honor system, and make every AI output traceable to its source so verification is one click, not a memo.
3
Protect 03 · Integrity
Actively Shape the Technology
Protect the sanctity of human decisions by holding our ethical boundaries.
Run every tool — new pitch or one you already own — through the value/risk test. Decline anything that can be talked around, that risks a career-ending mistake on a bad day, or that thinks for the human instead of returning the source.
4
Protect 04 · The System
Work Together
No office holds the line alone.
Ask police, defense, the court, and your vendors what their AI policies and use cases are. Use the questions in Appendix III. We are all changing the system at once; the standard only holds if we set it together.
Appendix I · Case Law

IMore Cases Worth Knowing

What's at stake: the public trust that empowers prosecution, the sanctity of human decisions, and the institution itself.

American public prosecution has stood for nearly four hundred years.† If we believe this work cannot be done by a machine, then we are the ones who have to defend it. None of the cases below are prosecution matters — the mechanics are identical. Each was a competent lawyer or agency that did not think it would happen to them. Tap to expand.

Jul 2016 · Wisc. Sup. Ct.State v. Loomis & COMPAS — a proprietary algorithm became a sentencing input, later found racially biased.+
The Original Sin of Legal AI

Eric Loomis was sentenced to six years partly on a COMPAS risk-assessment score. He challenged on due-process grounds: the algorithm was a trade secret he could not audit. The Wisconsin Supreme Court upheld the sentence but required defendants be informed of the tool's limitations. Two months earlier, ProPublica's analysis of 7,000 scores found COMPAS misclassified Black defendants as high-risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants.

A human decided, and signed off without being able to see the work. Every “AI for prosecutors” pitch since has had to argue around Loomis.

Source: ProPublica, “Machine Bias” (May 2016)
Jun 2023 · S.D.N.Y.Mata v. Avianca — $5,000 sanction. “I didn't know AI could lie” was rejected as a defense.+
The Original

Two attorneys filed a federal brief citing six ChatGPT-generated decisions that did not exist. When opposing counsel could not locate them, the attorney returned to ChatGPT, which assured him they were real and produced fake excerpts. Judge Castel imposed $5,000 in sanctions and required apology letters. “I trusted the AI” was rejected then and is still rejected. The signing attorney owns every citation.

Source: ABA Litigation News (2023)
Jan 2024 · 2d Cir.Park v. Kim — disciplinary referral. Two months later she did it again as a pro se litigant.+
Same Lawyer, Twice

Attorney Jae S. Lee cited a case that did not exist, having asked ChatGPT for help and not confirmed it. The court referred her to its Grievance Panel. Two months later, in her own pro se action, she filed a brief with additional hallucinated authority. The first sanction is a warning; the second is the pattern. Individual goodwill is not a substitute for an enforced office policy with no exceptions.

Source: Bloomberg Law (Jan 2024)
Feb 2025 · D. WyomingWadsworth v. Walmart — $5,000 in sanctions for hallucinations from the firm's in-house AI tool.+
It Is Not Just ChatGPT

Three attorneys cited eight cases that did not exist — generated by the firm's in-house AI research tool, not ChatGPT. The court fined the lead attorney $3,000, revoked his pro hac vice admission, and fined two others $1,000 each. Vendor logos do not move the responsibility. Enterprise and “law-firm-trained” tools hallucinate at meaningful rates.

Source: PlatinumIDS, AI Hallucination Crisis Report 2026
Jul 2025 · N.D. Ala.Johnson v. Dunn — sanctions against a well-regarded national firm in a routine motion.+
Big-Law Is Not Immune

A motion signed by attorneys at a large litigation firm cited multiple nonexistent cases. The court imposed sanctions, ordered remedial education, and referred the matter to bar authorities — explicitly rejecting any defense based on firm size, AI training, or verification policies. The verification gap is structural: at the keyboard, not the org chart.

Source: Esquire Deposition Solutions (Aug 2025)
Sep 2025 · CA Ct. App.Noland v. Land of the Free — $10,000 sanction. Published precedent in California.+
First Published California Opinion

The Court of Appeal issued California's first published opinion sanctioning AI hallucinations, fined the attorney $10,000, and made the holding binding statewide — the same state where Nevada County's DA office now faces Supreme Court review. The civil standard is set; the criminal standard is being built on top of it.

Source: Scientific American (May 2026)
Mar 2026 · 6th Cir.Whiting v. City of Athens — $30,000 + full fee shifting at the appellate level.+
What the High End Looks Like

Two attorneys filed three appellate briefs with more than two dozen fabricated or misrepresented citations. A unanimous panel imposed $15,000 per attorney (the statutory maximum), required full reimbursement of the opposing party's fees, and referred both for discipline. The trajectory is steeper than the 2023 cases suggested. The next ones will not be smaller.

Source: PlatinumIDS, AI Hallucination Crisis Report 2026

Appendix IV · The Whole System

IVSystemic AI

A prosecutor never operates in isolation. Every case touches police, victims, witnesses, defense, judges, jurors, experts, and the defendant — and each of them will reach for AI differently. The question is not whether AI is “good” or “bad.” It is, for each role: how is AI used, how does that use affect the prosecution, what ethical concerns arise, what practical problems should we anticipate, and what safeguards are needed.

Failure Mode · Compounding Error

§Problem: Model Collapse

More of what arrives in the office is already AI-written. Our tools summarize that output, and the next tool summarizes the summary. Each pass is a chance to drift, and the errors do not cancel out. They compound (model collapse).

Source · human
A human writes the original report. One ambiguous phrase: “found near the vehicle.”
AI summarizes
A tool tightens it to “found in the vehicle.” Cleaner, more confident, slightly wrong.
AI on AI
The next tool summarizes the summary, then the next. The hedge is gone; the error reads as fact.

Each pass feeds the next · the rare and the uncertain fade first · distortion compounds, it does not average out

Why It Happens
  • AI is built to sound confident, so hedges and rare details are smoothed away first.
  • So much incoming is already AI-written that our tools summarize other AI, not a human record.
  • Every summarization step is lossy. Stack three and the loss is invisible but real.
Why It Matters for Us
  • “Near the vehicle” becoming “in the vehicle” is the difference between a charge that holds and one that doesn't.
  • We may never see the original once three layers of summary sit on top.
  • The error arrives looking polished and certain — the hardest kind to catch.

Solution: Set the Terms

The prosecutor signs the document and answers for it in court. So the prosecutor sets the terms. For any AI-generated content, demand:

1
The Raw Source Travels With the Output
Every AI-generated piece arrives with the prompts that produced it and the primary sources it drew on. No source, no reliance.
2
Disclosed Failure History
The vendor tells you where the AI in their product has already caused legal problems. Silence is its own answer.
3
A Complete Authorship Audit Log
A full record of which content the AI generated and which a human wrote or edited, with timestamps and version history.
4
Visibility Into Whether We Are Still Rigorous
You can see whether your people are still verifying, or have drifted to clicking “accept, accept, accept.”
The Non-Negotiable

§Traceability or Disable the Feature

If a model writes it, you must be able to click it and see where it came from. Every AI-generated sentence should carry a link back to the primary source — one hover, one tap, the original document.

Case summaryAI-generated

The defendant was arrested on March 14, 2026 following a traffic stop. Officers reported that a controlled substance was found under the driver's seat. The defendant has one prior diversion, completed in 2023.

ⓘ Hover any underlined sentence to see the exact source line.

No sentence stands on its own word The summary is convenient, but the summary is not the evidence.
  • Sentence-level, not document-level. “It's in the file somewhere” is not traceability. The link lands on the line.
  • Verify before you rely. One hover confirms the claim or exposes the drift — “in the vehicle” vs. “near the vehicle.”
  • It survives the chain. Even after layers of summary, the trail back to the human-authored original stays intact.
  • Ask your vendor for it by name — Appendix III: “If I highlight a description the AI wrote, can I open the exact document it was pulled from?”
Appendix V · The Rubric

VHow to Rate an AI Feature

The question isn't “AI vs. no AI.” It's how the tool is built. The same feature name can hide very different builds. The next six sections each rate one feature's variations against two axes and four verdicts.

The Two Axes

  • Value — realistic time saved × how often you would use it. Not the marketing pitch.
  • Risk — harm if the output is wrong × probability the output is wrong on a typical day. Not the worst-case imaginable.

The Four Verdicts

  • Don'tThe build cannot be made safe by configuration. Reject regardless of vendor claims.
  • PilotThe build could be safe with specific safeguards. Run it with logging and sampled review before widening.
  • AdoptThe build is sound by design. Operate it and monitor.
  • TrickNo need for AI; the simpler solution wins.
The recurring shape of an honest Adopt AI extracts named facts from the source. The office writes a rule that turns those facts into an outcome. The same facts always produce the same outcome. Anyone can read the rule, and it only changes when the office changes it. Call this a data-based policy.

TaskDecide which incoming cases the diversion coordinator should review for eligibility. Walked end-to-end in Appendix II.

What This Build Does

v1.1+ ReasoningDon'tValue High · Risk High

The rationale rationalizes a conclusion the model already reached. The model is still doing the discretion.

v2.0Extract → PolicyAdoptValue High · Risk Med

What This Build Does

AI extracts a fixed list of facts from the report (each cited to a paragraph). The office writes a deterministic rule against those facts: IF violence=none AND priors=0 AND relationship IN (family, cohabitating) THEN flag=diversion-review.

  • Identical facts → identical flag.
  • Rule is auditable; office can change it deliberately.
  • Each fact traces to a source paragraph.

Cons

  • Extraction errors propagate.
  • Rule has gaps the office didn't anticipate.
  • Needs ongoing sample audit of unflagged cases.
Why Adopt

Structurally sound. AI fills facts; the office's written rule does the flagging. Log every extraction; sample-audit unflagged cases.

v2.1+ Pattern ScoutAdoptValue High · Risk Low

What This Build Does

Same fact-extraction + rule as v2.0. In parallel, AI clusters cases the rule did not flag and surfaces recurring fact patterns (e.g. 12 unflagged cases share housing-instability + first-offense). Suggestions queue for office review; nothing auto-applies.

Pros

  • Deterministic rule still gates.
  • AI is purely additive — only proposes.
  • Office decides which patterns become policy.
  • Surfaces edge cases the rule didn't anticipate.

Cons

  • Pattern suggestions can bias the coordinator toward the AI's clustering.
  • New fields must be reviewed for the disparity risks the rule was designed to avoid.
Why Adopt

The rule is the floor; AI only proposes additions; the office controls what changes.

What This Build Does

For a one-off email where no template fits, use a government-approved drafting assistant walled off from the case record. User provides context; model drafts; a human reviews and sends.

Pros

  • Handles edge cases templates miss.
  • CJIS / case-record data never enters the prompt.
  • Human still authors the send.

Cons

  • User may paste case facts into the prompt to “give context” — mitigated by training and the technical wall.
Why Adopt

Scope-limited use; case record stays out of the prompt; human authors the send.
